Overview — Background, Objectives & History of RBI

The Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 is the founding legislation that established the Reserve Bank of India. RBI formally commenced operations on 1st April 1935 in Kolkata; its headquarters shifted to Mumbai in 1937. Prior to RBI, the Imperial Bank of India (est. 1921) performed partial central banking functions, but this was inadequate for a growing economy.

The Hilton Young (Royal) Commission of 1926 first formally recommended a separate central bank for India. After years of deliberation and multiple drafts, the RBI Act was enacted in 1934. Initially RBI was a privately-owned shareholder institution. After Independence, the Government nationalized RBI on 1st January 1949 under the Reserve Bank of India (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act, 1948.

Primary Objectives of RBI (Preamble to RBI Act)
  • Regulate the issue of banknotes across India to ensure uniform currency
  • Maintain monetary stability in India — controlling inflation and price levels
  • Operate the currency and credit system to the country's advantage
  • Develop and maintain a modern monetary policy framework — added post-2016 MPC amendment to target inflation
  • Banker to Government — Central and State Governments
  • Banker's Bank — lender of last resort for the banking system
  • Manage India's foreign exchange reserves under FEMA 1999
📌 Exam TipRBI established 1 April 1935 at Kolkata | HQ shifted to Mumbai 1937 | Nationalized 1 January 1949 | Hilton Young Commission 1926 recommended RBI. These four facts appear in almost every exam paper.

Composition of the Central Board & Term of Office — Section 8

The Central Board of Directors under Section 8 of the RBI Act is the apex governing body. It is constituted by the Central Government and oversees RBI's management and policy.

MemberNumberAppointed ByTerm
Governor1Central Govt.As specified (usually 3 yrs, extendable)
Deputy GovernorsUp to 4Central Govt.As specified by Central Govt.
Nominated Directors (Sec 8(1)(c))10Central Govt.4 years; re-appointable
Govt. Official Directors (Sec 8(1)(d))2Central Govt.During pleasure of Central Govt.

Nominated Directors must collectively have expertise in: economics, commerce, agriculture, industry, finance, cooperative banking, small-scale industries. The 10 nominated directors represent diverse economic interests ensuring broad-based governance. Maximum board strength = 17 members.

Disqualifications of Directors
  • Insolvency — adjudicated insolvent or unable to pay debts
  • Criminal conviction involving moral turpitude
  • Unsound mind (declared by a competent court)
  • Absence from 3 consecutive Board meetings without leave of absence
  • Conflict of interest — holding a position in a banking/financial company that conflicts with RBI governance
  • Removed by Central Government before expiry of term on specific grounds

Local Boards — Section 9

Under Section 9, four Local Boards are established to provide regional representation in RBI's governance and advisory structure.

Each Local Board consists of 5 members appointed by the Central Government for 4 years. Members must have special knowledge of local conditions and experience in cooperative/indigenous banking, agriculture, rural economy, trade, or industry of the region.

Functions of Local Boards
  • Advise the Central Board on matters referred to them by the Central Board
  • Perform duties delegated to them by the Central Board
  • Represent regional and territorial interests in RBI's policy considerations
  • Act as liaison between local banking needs and Central Board decisions
  • Examine proposals for banking expansion and new branch openings in their region
  • Provide inputs on regional economic conditions for monetary policy formulation
📌 Exam Tip4 Local Boards — Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, New Delhi. 5 members each, 4-year term. Functions: purely advisory and delegated — they do NOT independently make policy decisions. A common MCQ trap.

Meetings of the Central Board & Important Committees

The Central Board meets regularly to govern RBI's operations. Key rules for meetings:

  • Must meet at least 6 times a year, at least once each quarter
  • Presided by the Governor; in absence, by a designated Deputy Governor
  • Decisions by majority vote; the presiding officer has a casting vote in case of tie
  • A minimum quorum of members must be present for valid resolutions
  • Minutes of all meetings are recorded and circulated
  • Extraordinary meetings may be called by the Governor or on requisition of a specified number of Directors

Business Bank May Transact — Section 17 & May NOT Transact — Section 19
Permitted Business — Section 17
Banking & Lending
  • Accept deposits from Govt., banks, FIs
  • Purchase, sell, discount bills of exchange
  • Grant loans to scheduled banks & State Govts.
  • Purchase and sell G-Secs
  • Open current accounts for banks and governments
  • Act as banker and agent to Central Govt.
  • Issue/manage government securities (public debt)
Currency & External
  • Issue, exchange, and cancel banknotes
  • Buy and sell gold coins and bullion
  • Hold and manage foreign exchange reserves
  • Open accounts in foreign/central banks abroad
  • Purchase/sell foreign exchange with authorized dealers
  • Manage IMF & World Bank transactions for GOI
  • Issue promissory notes/cheques payable to bearer
Prohibited Business — Section 19
  • No trading in goods — cannot buy/sell goods or engage in commercial trading activity
  • No share purchases — cannot hold equity in private companies
  • No mortgage loans — cannot advance money on security of immovable property
  • No personal security loans — cannot lend purely on personal guarantee without collateral
  • No unsecured advances to public — all lending must be to scheduled banks or State Govts. and be secured
  • No speculative activity — cannot fund speculative ventures (derivatives speculation, commodity trading)
  • No retail banking — cannot accept deposits from or lend directly to the general public

These prohibitions ensure RBI functions as a pure central bank and monetary authority, not as a commercial entity competing with banks it regulates. Commercial activity would compromise its regulatory independence and credibility.

📌 Exam TipSection 17 = CAN do | Section 19 = CANNOT do. Key prohibition to remember: RBI cannot purchase shares of any company, cannot lend to general public, cannot trade in goods. This contrast is among the most frequently tested in MCQs and short answers.
06
Right to Issue Banknotes, Denominations & Forms of Bank Notes — Section 22

Section 22 confers upon RBI the sole and exclusive right to issue banknotes in India. This monopoly is the bedrock of RBI's role as currency issuer and ensures uniformity, trust, and control over money supply.

The Re. 1 Exception

The only exception is the One Rupee (Re. 1) note, issued by the Ministry of Finance, Government of India, bearing the signature of the Finance Secretary (not RBI Governor). Similarly, all coins are minted under the Coinage Act by the Government of India, though RBI handles their distribution.

Denominations Authorized
  • Currently active: ₹2, ₹5, ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500
  • Maximum denomination permitted by law: ₹10,000 per note
  • ₹2000 notes: Announced for withdrawal in May 2023; still legal tender until deposited
  • Demonetized in 2016: ₹500 and ₹1000 old series (8 November 2016)
  • Demonetized in 1978: ₹1000, ₹5000, ₹10,000 by Morarji Desai government
Forms of Bank Notes — Key Mandatory Features
  • Every note carries a guarantee by the Central Government of India
  • Bears the signature of the RBI Governor (exception: Re.1 — Finance Secretary)
  • Contains the fundamental promise: "I promise to pay the bearer the sum of ___" — reflecting notes as RBI's liability
  • Must contain mandatory security features: watermarks, security thread, microprinting, UV features, intaglio printing (raised printing for visually impaired)
  • Printing locations: Currency Note Press Nashik, Bank Note Press Dewas (Govt. of India via SPMCIL); RBI's own presses at Mysore and Salboni (West Bengal)
📌 Exam TipSection 22 = RBI's sole right to issue notes. Exception = Re.1 note (Finance Ministry). Maximum denomination = ₹10,000. All notes bear Governor's signature except Re.1. 4 printing locations: Nashik, Dewas, Mysore, Salboni.

Legal Tender Character — Section 26 & Demonetization — Section 26(2)
Legal Tender — Section 26
Every banknote issued by RBI shall be legal tender at par (at face value) at any place in India in payment of any amount. A creditor cannot legally refuse RBI notes in settlement of a debt denominated in that currency.
TypeLegal Tender StatusNotes
RBI Banknotes (₹2 and above)Unlimited Legal TenderMust be accepted for any amount
Re.1 Note (Govt. of India)Unlimited Legal TenderIssued by Finance Ministry
Coins (₹1 to ₹20)Limited Legal TenderCoinage Act limits for large quantities
Demonetization — Section 26(2)

Under Section 26(2), on the recommendation of the Central Board of RBI, the Central Government may by notification declare that any series/denomination of notes shall cease to be legal tender from a specified date. This is Demonetization.

  • 1978: ₹1000, ₹5000, ₹10,000 notes demonetized by Morarji Desai government
  • 8 November 2016: ₹500 and ₹1000 (old Mahatma Gandhi series) demonetized by Modi government — largest demonetization in Indian history; 86% of currency by value withdrawn overnight
  • 2023: ₹2000 notes announced for withdrawal (not demonetized — remain legal tender till deposited)
📌 Exam TipSection 26 = legal tender. Section 26(2) = demonetization power (Central Govt. on RBI Central Board recommendation). 2016 demonetization = ₹500 + ₹1000 = 86% of currency by value. The distinction between "withdrawal" (₹2000 in 2023) and "demonetization" (2016) is important.

Cease, Reissue, Mutilated, Stolen, Counterfeit Notes & Special Bank Notes
Reissue of Notes

Notes returned to RBI (from banks or currency chests) can be reissued if they pass fitness criteria — not torn, not heavily soiled, not counterfeit, and within the issuable life. Notes failing fitness criteria are mechanically shredded and destroyed at currency chests under dual control.

Mutilated and Soiled Notes — RBI Note Refund Policy
Condition of NoteEntitlementWhere
Soiled / Slightly worn (complete)Full face valueAny bank branch
Mutilated — over half the note intact and serial number visibleFull valueRBI Issue Office or designated banks
Mutilated — specific area requirement met (between half and required minimum)Proportional (half) valueRBI Issue Office only
Less than required minimum areaNo value — rejectedNot exchangeable
Stolen Notes

RBI is not liable to pay the value of stolen currency notes. Currency notes are bearer instruments — whoever holds them is presumed to be the owner. Banks are not required to compensate customers for stolen currency. Recovery of printing cost for stolen notes is not entertained.

Counterfeit Notes
  • Counterfeit notes must be impounded by banks — NOT returned to the depositor
  • Bank must stamp "COUNTERFEIT NOTE" on it and provide the depositor a receipt (not the note)
  • For counterfeits of denomination ₹5 and above, banks must lodge an FIR with police
  • Counterfeit notes are forwarded to the nearest Forged Note Vigilance Cell or police
  • No value is paid to depositor of counterfeit notes
Provision for Issue of Special Bank Notes

In extraordinary circumstances (war, extreme financial emergency, disruption of normal banking), the Central Government may authorize RBI to issue Special Bank Notes or Special One-Rupee Notes. These carry the same legal tender status as regular notes during their validity period. This provision ensures monetary system flexibility during national crises.

📌 Exam TipStolen notes = RBI not liable. Counterfeit = impounded + FIR (for ₹5 and above). Mutilated = proportional value based on area of note. Three key distinctions frequently tested together.

Powers of Central Govt. to Supersede Central Board — Section 30

Section 30 empowers the Central Government to supersede the Central Board of RBI under extreme circumstances. This reflects the ultimate sovereignty of the elected government over public institutions.

Grounds for Supersession
  • The Central Board persistently defaults in complying with requirements imposed by the RBI Act
  • The Board is unable to perform its duties for any reason
  • Supersession is necessary in the public interest
  • Cases of financial mismanagement or gross neglect of duty
Consequences of Supersession
  • All Directors vacate their offices immediately upon notification in the Official Gazette
  • An Administrator is appointed by the Central Government to manage RBI
  • Administrator exercises all powers of the Central Board
  • Maximum period: 6 months at a time; extendable to a total of 12 months
  • After the supersession period ends, a fresh reconstituted Central Board takes over
  • The notification of supersession must be published in the Official Gazette
📌 Exam TipSection 30 = supersession of Central Board. Max 6 months, extendable to 12 months total. Administrator appointed by Central Govt. This provision has NEVER been invoked in practice — but is extremely important for theory examinations.

Cash Reserves (CRR), SLR & Monetary Policy Instruments
Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)
CRR — Section 42, RBI Act
The percentage of a scheduled bank's Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that must be maintained as cash with RBI. It is a direct and powerful monetary policy instrument.
  • NDTL = (Demand liabilities + Time liabilities) MINUS inter-bank assets
  • Maintained on a daily average basis over a fortnightly (14-day) reporting period
  • Banks earn NO interest on CRR balances maintained with RBI
  • Penalty for shortfall: 3% above Bank Rate per annum on shortfall amount for each day of default
  • Raising CRR → absorbs liquidity → reduces money supply → controls inflation
  • Lowering CRR → injects liquidity → increases money supply → stimulates growth
Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) — Section 24, BR Act
  • Percentage of NDTL that banks must maintain in liquid assets with themselves (not with RBI)
  • Eligible SLR assets: Cash, Gold, Approved Securities (primarily G-Secs, Treasury Bills, SDLs)
  • Banks earn interest on SLR assets (unlike CRR)
  • Maximum SLR = 40% of NDTL; Statutory minimum = 25% of NDTL
  • Purpose: (1) ensures basic liquidity, (2) provides captive market for government borrowings at lower rates
Key Monetary Policy Rate Instruments
InstrumentDefinitionEffect
Repo RateRate at which RBI lends short-term funds to banks against G-Secs (repurchase agreement). Set by MPC.Benchmark lending rate; rise = credit costly = less inflation; fall = cheaper credit = more growth
Reverse Repo RateRate at which banks park excess funds with RBI overnight (Standing Deposit Facility since 2022)Absorbs excess liquidity from system; typically Repo −25 bps
Bank RateRate at which RBI lends long-term / rediscounts eligible bills; linked to Repo RateUsed as penalty rate benchmark for CRR/SLR shortfall calculations
Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)Emergency overnight borrowing window for banks against their SLR securitiesSafety valve for sudden liquidity needs; typically Repo +25 bps
Open Market Operations (OMO)RBI buys/sells G-Secs in open marketOMO purchase = injects liquidity; OMO sale = absorbs liquidity
Variable Rate Repo (VRR)Short-term variable rate liquidity injection through auctionFine-tunes daily/weekly liquidity conditions
📌 Exam TipCRR = held with RBI, NO interest earned. SLR = held by bank, EARNS interest on G-Secs. Repo = RBI lends to banks. Reverse Repo / SDF = banks lend to RBI. MSF = emergency borrowing (above SLR securities). These distinctions are fundamental and appear in every paper.

Credit Information, Non-Banking Institutions & Winding Up Powers
Collection & Furnishing of Credit Information — Chapter IIIA (Sections 45A–45F)
  • Credit Information = any information relating to amounts and nature of credit facilities granted to any borrower by a credit institution
  • Banks are obligated to furnish credit information to RBI as and when required
  • RBI can furnish credit information to any credit institution in consolidated, non-individual form
  • Information is confidential — cannot be disclosed except to authorized persons
  • This Chapter laid the legislative groundwork for the Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005 and modern credit bureaus: CIBIL, Equifax, CRIF Highmark, Experian India
  • CIBIL scores (300–900) are now the standard metric for individual creditworthiness in Indian banking
Powers over Non-Banking Institutions — Section 45C
  • RBI can direct any NBFC or non-banking institution to submit returns, statements, and reports about financial position, assets, liabilities, credit transactions
  • Covers: loan companies, investment companies, asset finance companies, microfinance institutions, chit fund companies
  • Systemically Important NBFCs (assets ≥ ₹500 crore) face stricter reporting and capital requirements
  • Failure to furnish information = monetary penalties imposed by RBI
  • RBI can conduct on-site inspections and off-site surveillance of NBFCs
  • RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework for NBFCs (2022) mirrors the framework for banks
Power of Bank to File Winding Up Petition

RBI can apply to the High Court for winding up of a banking company if:

  • Banking company is unable to pay its debts
  • Has ceased to carry on banking business
  • Is carrying on banking in a manner detrimental to depositors' interests
  • Has failed to maintain required CRR/SLR reserves
  • Continued existence is against public interest

Practical example: Punjab & Maharashtra Co-operative Bank (PMC Bank) crisis (2019) — RBI placed restrictions on the bank, limiting withdrawals, to protect depositors. PMC Bank was ultimately merged with Unity Small Finance Bank (2022).

📌 Exam TipChapter IIIA = credit information collection — forerunner of CIBIL (2005). RBI files winding-up petition with High Court — it does not directly wind up a bank. PMC Bank 2019 is an excellent practical example to cite in answers.

RBI as Banker to Government, Banker's Bank & Foreign Exchange
Banker to the Government — Sections 20 & 21
  • RBI is the statutory banker, agent, and financial adviser to the Central Government (mandatory); State Governments by agreement
  • Maintains government accounts and makes payments on government's behalf
  • Manages public debt — issues and redeems G-Secs, T-Bills, SDLs through the Debt Management Cell
  • Provides Ways and Means Advances (WMA) to bridge temporary gaps between government revenue and expenditure
  • Acts as fiscal agent — collects taxes, makes transfer payments, handles provident fund receipts
  • Represents India at IMF, World Bank, BIS meetings
RBI as Banker's Bank — Lender of Last Resort
  • Maintains current accounts of all scheduled commercial banks — interbank settlements happen through these accounts
  • Provides emergency liquidity through Repo window, MSF window to banks facing sudden shortages
  • Acts as lender of last resort — prevents bank runs from causing systemic collapse
  • Custodian of banks' CRR balances
  • Operates RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement) — the backbone for all large-value interbank settlements
  • Sets and enforces Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework for weak banks
Foreign Exchange Management — FEMA 1999
  • RBI manages India's Foreign Exchange Reserves (among world's top 5; crossed $700 billion in 2024)
  • Regulates current account transactions (trade payments, remittances) — largely liberalized; convertible on current account since 1994
  • Regulates capital account transactions (FDI, FPI, ECB, NRI deposits) — partially regulated
  • Manages exchange rate through market interventions — India follows a managed float system (not fully fixed, not fully free)
  • Licenses and regulates Authorized Dealers (ADs) — Category I (banks), Category II (money changers) who deal in foreign exchange
  • FEMA 1999 replaced FERA 1973 — shift from criminal to civil offence for most violations
📌 Exam TipFEMA 1999 replaced FERA 1973. India has managed float exchange rate (not fixed, not free float). Ways and Means Advances = temporary overdraft to Govt. Foreign exchange reserves = managed by RBI, invested in G-Secs of advanced economies, gold, SDRs.

Quick Revision Summary

The RBI Act 1934 established India's central bank (1 April 1935, nationalized 1949), as recommended by the Hilton Young Commission (1926). The Central Board (max 17 members: Governor + 4 DGs + 10 Directors + 2 Govt. officials) governs RBI, supported by 4 regional Local Boards (5 members each, 4 years). The MPC (6 members since 2016) sets the Repo Rate targeting 4% CPI ±2% inflation. Section 22 gives RBI the exclusive right to issue banknotes (exception: Re.1 by Finance Ministry); Section 26 makes them legal tender; Section 26(2) enables demonetization on RBI Central Board recommendation. The Issue Department maintains assets (gold + G-Secs) backing all notes in circulation under the Minimum Reserve System (₹200 crore minimum since 1957). CRR (held with RBI, no interest) and SLR (held by bank, earns interest, max 40%) are core monetary tools alongside Repo, Bank Rate, MSF, and OMOs. Chapter IIIA enables credit information collection — the forerunner of CIBIL. Section 30 allows Central Govt. to supersede the Board (max 12 months total). RBI is Banker to Government (Sections 20–21), Banker's Bank (lender of last resort through RTGS), and manages India's forex reserves under FEMA 1999 (replaced FERA 1973).

01
Overview of Banking Regulation Act, 1949

The Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (BR Act) is the principal comprehensive legislation governing the regulation and supervision of banking companies in India. It came into force on 16 March 1949, replacing the earlier inadequate provisions of the Companies Act, 1913.

Before the BR Act, several major bank failures in the 1940s (notably the Palai Central Bank failure in Kerala, 1960 exposed the gap) demonstrated the need for a sector-specific law. The BR Act empowers RBI as the primary regulator of all aspects of bank governance.

Scope and Application
CategoryApplicability
Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs)Fully applicable
Foreign Banks operating in IndiaApplicable with modifications (e.g., branch-level capital requirements)
Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)Applicable
Urban Co-operative Banks & State Co-operative BanksApplicable since Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020
Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs)NOT covered (governed by Chapter III-B of RBI Act)

The Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020 is a landmark reform that brought all cooperative banks (urban and state) under RBI's supervision — triggered by the PMC Bank crisis of 2019, where cooperative banking's weak regulation allowed fraud to go undetected for years.

📌 Exam TipBR Act enacted 16 March 1949. 2020 Amendment = cooperative banks under RBI. NBFCs = NOT under BR Act (under RBI Act Chapter III-B). These distinctions are extremely frequently tested.
02
Key Definitions — Section 5
Banking — Section 5(b)
"Accepting, for the purpose of lending or investment, of deposits of money from the public, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order or otherwise." Three essential elements: (1) accepting deposits (2) from the PUBLIC (3) for the purpose of lending or investment. All three must be present.
Banking Company — Section 5(c)
"Any company which transacts the business of banking in India." The company must be actively carrying on banking business (as defined in 5(b)) in India. A company that only accepts deposits but does not lend would not be a banking company.
Demand Liabilities — Section 5(f)
Liabilities which must be met on demand — current account balances, savings account balances, overdue fixed deposits, outstanding demand drafts. These form the "D" in NDTL.
Time Liabilities — Section 5(g)
Liabilities not repayable on demand — fixed deposits, recurring deposits, cash certificates, bonds, certificates of deposit (maturity-based). These form the "T" in NDTL.
Approved Securities — Section 5(a)
Securities in which a trustee may invest money under the Indian Trusts Act — primarily Government of India securities and state government securities. Critical for SLR eligibility.
Secured Loan — Section 5(n)
A loan or advance made on the security of assets of the borrower whose market value is not at any time less than the amount of the loan or advance.
📌 Exam TipThe three-part definition of Banking (Section 5(b)) is the most tested definition: deposits + from public + for lending/investment. All three elements are necessary. Accepting deposits from friends only = NOT banking. Accepting deposits for safekeeping (not lending) = NOT banking.
03
Business of Banking Companies — Section 6

Section 6 lists all forms of business a banking company may lawfully engage in. The core business under Section 5(b) is supplemented by a wide range of ancillary and related activities.

Core Financial Services
  • Borrowing, raising, taking up money
  • Lending and advancing money (secured or unsecured)
  • Buying/selling bills of exchange, hundis, promissory notes, drafts
  • Granting and issuing letters of credit, travellers' cheques, circular notes
  • Buying and selling bullion and specie
  • Buying and selling foreign exchange
  • Acquiring, holding, issuing on commission or otherwise, stocks/shares/bonds/debentures
Agency & Advisory Services
  • Collecting and transmitting money and securities
  • Safe custody of valuables (locker services)
  • Acting as agent, correspondent, representative for other banks/financial institutions
  • Undertaking and executing trusts
  • Carrying on and transacting guarantee and indemnity business
  • Managing property for satisfaction of bank's claims
  • Carrying on business as permitted by Section 6(1)(o) — residuary clause

Section 6(1)(o) Residuary Clause: "Any other form of business which the Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, specify as a form of business in which it is lawful for a banking company to engage." This has been used to permit banks to distribute insurance products, sell mutual funds, and offer pension products — but only through separately capitalized subsidiaries or specifically notified arrangements.

📌 Exam TipSection 6 = what banks CAN do. Insurance and mutual funds are NOT directly permitted under Section 6 — banks do these via separately incorporated subsidiaries. Example: SBI Life Insurance, HDFC Life are subsidiaries, not bank departments.
04
Prohibition of Trading, Disposal of Non-Banking Assets & Managing Agents
Prohibition of Trading — Section 8

A banking company is prohibited from directly or indirectly buying, selling, or bartering goods. Banking is a business of trust — depositor funds must not be exposed to commercial trading risk.

  • Banks CANNOT trade in physical commodities (metals, agri-goods, manufactured goods) for profit
  • Banks CAN hold goods as collateral security for a loan
  • Banks CAN sell goods received as collateral after loan default — but must do so within a specified time period
  • Buying/selling of foreign exchange and government securities = NOT trading (these are financial instruments, not goods)
  • Penalty for violation: RBI can cancel banking licence; criminal proceedings under BR Act
Disposal of Non-Banking Assets — Section 9

A banking company cannot hold any immovable property (land, buildings) except for its own use as bank premises for a period exceeding 7 years from the date of acquisition.

  • This applies to property acquired through debt recovery (auction purchase, mortgage enforcement)
  • The 7-year limit can be extended by RBI in individual cases with sufficient cause
  • Purpose: prevent banks from becoming real estate companies and ensure non-productive assets are converted to cash for relending
  • Non-banking assets must be reported separately in the bank's balance sheet with acquisition date
Employment of Managing Agents — Section 10

Banking companies are absolutely prohibited from employing managing agents. Before 1949, many Indian banks were controlled by managing agency houses (e.g., the Marwari and Parsi trading houses) that made banking decisions in their own interests. Section 10 eliminated this conflict of interest completely.

📌 Exam TipSection 8 = no trading in goods. Section 9 = non-banking assets must be sold within 7 years (extendable by RBI). Section 10 = managing agents ABSOLUTELY PROHIBITED. All three section numbers are directly asked in MCQs.
05
Board of Directors, Managerial Appointments & RBI's Governance Powers
Board Composition Requirements — Section 10A
  • At least 51% of directors must have special knowledge or practical experience in one or more of: accountancy, agriculture, small-scale industry, banking, cooperative movement, finance, law, economics
  • A director cannot hold directorship in more than one banking company at the same time (prevents conflict of interest)
  • Not less than 2/3rd of directors must be persons whose period of office is not liable to determination by rotation (ensures continuity of expertise on the board)
Appointment of MD/CEO — Section 10B
  • Appointment, re-appointment, or termination of Managing Director (MD) or Chief Executive Officer (CEO) requires prior approval of RBI
  • A person cannot simultaneously be the Chairman of the Board AND the MD/CEO (separation of powers principle)
  • Section 10BB: RBI can direct removal and appointment of a new MD/CEO in public interest or in interest of depositors
Fit and Proper Criteria — Section 10C

All directors and senior management of banks must meet RBI's Fit and Proper criteria (Fit and Proper Policy):

  • Integrity and track record: No criminal convictions, no significant adverse regulatory findings
  • Competence: Sufficient educational and professional qualifications relevant to their role
  • Financial soundness: No bankruptcy; adequate personal financial standing
  • No conflict of interest: No significant interests in competing entities
  • Board must annually review Fit and Proper status of all directors
Power to Remove Managerial Persons — Section 36AA

RBI can remove any Chairman, Director, CEO, Officer, or Employee if:

  • The person is causing or likely to cause damage to depositors' interests
  • Has been convicted of a criminal offence involving moral turpitude
  • Has become subject to any disqualification under the Act
  • RBI is satisfied it is necessary in public interest
Power to Appoint Additional Directors — Section 36AB

When RBI considers it necessary in public interest or interest of depositors, it may appoint one or more additional directors on the board of any banking company. These directors hold office for such period as RBI specifies and exercise all powers of regular directors. This was used during YES Bank's restructuring (2020) when RBI placed a moratorium and appointed administrator/directors.

📌 Exam TipSection 10A: 51% directors must have special knowledge. Section 10B: MD/CEO appointment needs RBI approval. Section 36AA: RBI can remove directors. Section 36AB: RBI can appoint additional directors. These four provisions make RBI the de facto super-regulator of bank governance.

Beyond statutory minimums, RBI requires banks to maintain a Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR / CAR) under Basel III:

07
Voting Rights, Dividends, Commission & Reserve Fund
Restriction on Voting Rights — Section 12A

In a banking company, no shareholder — individual or institutional — can exercise voting rights exceeding 10% of total voting rights regardless of the number of shares held. Purpose: prevent monopolization of bank governance.

  • Even if a person holds 30% shares, their voting right is capped at 10%
  • For promoters of private banks, RBI has a glide path: initial holding allowed up to 40%, phased down to 15% over 15 years (RBI guidelines, not the Act itself)
  • Government-owned PSU banks are effectively exempt from this cap due to their ownership structure
  • Purpose: ensures no single entity can control a bank and undermine governance
Restrictions on Commission, Brokerage, Discount — Section 13

A banking company cannot pay commission, brokerage, discount, or remuneration on the issue of its shares at a rate exceeding 2.5% of paid-up value of shares. This prevents excessive capital drain during share issuances.

Restriction on Payment of Dividends — Section 15
  • A bank CANNOT declare or pay dividends until all capitalized expenses (preliminary expenses, share issuance commission, brokerage, discount on shares) are fully written off
  • Banks must also comply with RBI's annual Dividend Distribution Policy
  • Current RBI guideline (2022): Banks with CRAR ≥ 11.5% and Net NPA < 6% can declare dividend; payout ratio depends on financial indicators
  • Purpose: prevents banks from distributing profits while carrying fictitious assets on books
Reserve Fund (Statutory Reserve) — Section 17

Every banking company MUST transfer a minimum of 20% of its net profit each year to a Reserve Fund (Statutory Reserve) BEFORE declaring any dividend.

  • This is a statutory obligation — not optional
  • Transfer continues until Reserve Fund equals paid-up capital
  • Once Reserve Fund = Paid-up Capital, further transfer may be exempted with RBI approval
  • Purpose: builds a permanent financial buffer to absorb unexpected future losses
  • In practice, well-run banks continue transferring even after Reserve Fund exceeds paid-up capital (conservative approach)
📌 Exam TipSection 12A = voting rights cap at 10%. Section 13 = commission/brokerage max 2.5%. Section 15 = write off capitalized expenses before dividend. Section 17 = 20% of net profit to Reserve Fund BEFORE dividend. All four section numbers are directly tested.
08
Cash Reserve (Section 18), SLR (Section 24) & Subsidiary Restrictions (Section 19)
Cash Reserve for Non-Scheduled Banks — Section 18

Section 18 requires every non-scheduled bank to maintain a cash reserve of at least 3% of its demand and time liabilities (DTL) at the close of business on any day.

  • This reserve can be maintained as: cash in hand (vault cash) OR as a balance with RBI or SBI
  • Non-scheduled banks submit returns to RBI showing compliance
  • Scheduled banks maintain CRR under Section 42 of the RBI Act (not Section 18 of BR Act) — important distinction
Statutory Liquidity Ratio — Section 24
  • Every banking company must maintain a minimum level of liquid assets equal to a specified percentage of its NDTL at the close of business every day
  • Eligible SLR assets: Cash, Gold valued at a price not exceeding current market price, Approved Securities (G-Secs, T-Bills, SDLs)
  • SLR assets are held by the bank itself (unlike CRR with RBI)
  • Banks earn interest on G-Secs held as SLR
  • Maximum SLR = 40% of NDTL (statutory ceiling); Minimum = 25% of NDTL (statutory floor)
  • Penalty for SLR shortfall: 3% above Bank Rate per annum on shortfall for each day of default
Restriction on Subsidiary Companies — Section 19
  • Banks can form subsidiaries only for permitted activities: trustee and estate administration, banking business in other countries, and with RBI approval: insurance, housing finance, mutual fund management, merchant banking, stock broking
  • Bank's holding in any company cannot exceed 30% of bank's own paid-up capital + reserves OR 30% of that company's paid-up capital — whichever is less
  • Purpose: maintains separation between banking and commerce; prevents banks from having dominant stakes in industrial companies
  • Practice: SBI Life Insurance, SBI Mutual Fund, HDFC Securities are separate subsidiaries — banks don't directly conduct these businesses
📌 Exam TipSection 18 = 3% cash reserve for NON-SCHEDULED banks only. Section 24 = SLR for ALL banks (25–40% NDTL); SLR assets = Cash + Gold + Approved Securities. Section 19 = subsidiary shareholding capped at 30% of bank's capital or company's capital (whichever less).

RBI Powers to Control Advances — Sections 21 & 35A
Section 21 — Specific Directions on Loans and Advances

RBI can issue directions to all banking companies regarding loans and advances:

  • Purpose restrictions: Specify purposes for which advances may or may not be made (e.g., no loans for speculative activity; sectoral credit caps)
  • Margin requirements: Specify minimum margins against secured loans (e.g., minimum 25% margin for gold loans means bank lends max 75% of gold value)
  • Exposure limits: Large Exposure Framework — single borrower limit = 25% of Tier 1 capital; group = 40% of Tier 1 capital
  • Priority Sector Lending (PSL): Banks must lend 40% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to priority sectors — Agriculture (18%), MSMEs, housing, education, social infrastructure, renewable energy
  • Rate of interest: Though banks now set rates under MCLR/External Benchmark Linked Rate (EBLR) system, RBI can issue directives on interest rate transparency
Section 35A — Omnibus Power to Issue Directions

Section 35A is RBI's broadest regulatory power. RBI can issue directions to any banking company if satisfied that it is necessary:

  • In the public interest
  • In the interest of banking policy
  • To prevent affairs of any banking company from being conducted detrimental to depositor interests
  • For securing proper management of any banking company

Section 35A has been used to issue: Master Directions on KYC, Cybersecurity Framework, Prudential Norms on Asset Classification and Provisioning (NPA recognition), Capital Adequacy guidelines, and the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework.

Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework

RBI's PCA Framework (based on Section 35A) imposes graduated restrictions on weak banks based on three trigger indicators:

  • Capital (CRAR): Below thresholds → restrictions on dividends, branch expansion, management compensation
  • Asset Quality (Net NPA ratio): Above thresholds → restrictions on new loans in risky categories
  • Profitability (Return on Assets): Consecutive years of losses → restrictions on capital expenditure
📌 Exam TipSection 21 = specific advance directions (PSL, margins, exposure limits). Section 35A = omnibus power for any direction in public interest. PSL = 40% of ANBC. PCA Framework = capital, NPA, profitability triggers. Large Exposure: 25% Tier 1 for single borrower.

The Banking Regulation Act, 1949 comprehensively governs banking companies in India (including co-operative banks since 2020 amendment; NBFCs excluded). Banking is defined as accepting deposits from the PUBLIC for lending/investment (Section 5(b) — all 3 elements must coexist). Introduction to IT Act, 2000 — Background & Structure

The Information Technology Act, 2000 (IT Act) was enacted on 9 June 2000, based on the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce, 1996 (United Nations Commission on International Trade Law). It was significantly amended by the IT (Amendment) Act, 2008 (effective from 27 October 2009) to address emerging cyber crimes, data protection, and intermediary liability.

Why IT Act Matters for Banking
  • Provides legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures — makes paperless banking legally valid
  • Enables electronic contracts (online loan agreements, internet banking T&Cs are legally binding)
  • Creates legal framework for digital payments — net banking, UPI, mobile banking
  • Defines cyber offences and penalties to protect banks and customers from fraud
  • Establishes the Certifying Authority framework for DSCs used in banking

Key Definitions & Communication Networks in Banking
Electronic Record — Section 2(t)
Data, record, or data generated, image, sound stored, received or sent in an electronic form or microfilm or computer-generated microfiche. In banking: e-statements, digital loan agreements, e-KYC records, digitally signed RTGS transactions.
Digital Signature — Section 2(p)
Authentication of any electronic record by a subscriber by means of an electronic method in accordance with Section 3. Uses asymmetric cryptography — unique to the signer, non-repudiable, and tamper-evident.
Electronic Signature — Section 2(ta) [Added by 2008 Amendment]
A broader category than Digital Signature. Includes any authentication technique notified by the Central Government — covers OTP-based authentication, Aadhaar-based e-sign, biometric authentication. More flexible; used for most online banking authentications.
Asymmetric Crypto System — Section 2(f)
A system of a secure key pair: a Private Key (used by subscriber to create digital signature) and a Public Key (used by verifier to verify the signature). Foundation of PKI (Public Key Infrastructure). Used in all secure banking: HTTPS, digital signatures on loan documents, SWIFT authentication.
Certifying Authority (CA) — Section 2(g)
A person granted a licence to issue Digital Signature Certificates under Section 24. In India: NIC (National Informatics Centre), eMudhra, NSDL e-Gov, Capricorn, and IDRBT (Institute for Development and Research in Banking Technology — set up by RBI).

01
Background — NPA Problem & Need for SARFAESI

Before SARFAESI, banks in India faced a severe Non-Performing Asset (NPA) crisis. Recovery through civil courts was extremely slow (often 10–15 years), and recovery rates were dismal — often less than 25 paise per rupee of NPA, far worse than international standards.

Problems Before SARFAESI
  • Banks had to file civil suits in District Courts for loan recovery — courts were massively backlogged
  • Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs), established in 1993 under RDDBFI Act, provided faster recovery but were still procedurally slow and borrowers could obtain stay orders easily
  • Borrowers could obtain ex-parte stay orders from civil courts, stalling bank recovery for years while continuing to use the assets
  • By the time courts decided, asset values had eroded significantly (machinery obsolete, property encumbered, business wound down)
  • Gross NPA ratio of Indian banks exceeded 15% in early 2000s — a major systemic risk
  • Banks became risk-averse — reluctant to lend to industry, hampering credit flow
Recommendations Leading to SARFAESI
  • Narasimham Committee-II (1998): Recommended a law enabling banks to enforce security without court intervention — modeled on UK, USA, and Germany's similar laws
  • Andhyarujina Committee: Specifically recommended creating the securitization and asset reconstruction framework
  • These recommendations led to the SARFAESI Act being enacted in 2002
📌 Exam TipSARFAESI recommended by Narasimham Committee II (1998) and Andhyarujina Committee. DRTs were established in 1993 (RDDBFI Act) — a precursor to SARFAESI. Background context is frequently asked in 10-mark questions.

Full Form, Objectives, Salient Features & Three Pillars
Full Form of SARFAESI Act, 2002
Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002. Memory aid: S = Securitisation | AR = Assets Reconstruction | F = Financial Assets | E = Enforcement | SI = Security Interest.
Objectives of SARFAESI Act
  • Enable banks to enforce security without court intervention — primary revolutionary objective
  • Create framework for securitization of financial assets to raise capital and remove NPAs from balance sheets
  • Establish Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) for specialized NPA resolution
  • Create CERSAI (Central Registry) to prevent fraudulent multiple mortgages
  • Improve India's overall credit culture by strengthening creditor rights and reducing willful defaulters
Three Pillars of SARFAESI
Pillar 1: Securitization
  • Converts illiquid loan pools into tradable securities
  • Raises fresh capital via Security Receipts (SRs)
  • Removes NPAs from bank balance sheet
  • SR investors get cash flows from underlying loans
  • Managed through SPV (trust or company)
Pillar 2: Asset Reconstruction
  • ARCs purchase NPAs from banks at discounted price
  • ARCs deploy specialized skills to revive/recover
  • Banks get upfront funds; ARCs bear recovery risk
  • ARCs can restructure, convert debt to equity, or sell
  • ARCs registered with and regulated by RBI
Pillar 3: Enforcement of Security Interest (The Most Important)
  • Banks take possession of secured assets WITHOUT court order (after 60-day notice)
  • Biggest change from pre-SARFAESI era
  • Applicable for secured NPA loans above ₹1 lakh
  • Borrowers have appeal rights: DRT within 45 days → DRAT → HC
  • Resulted in significant improvement in NPA recovery rates
📌 Exam TipThe full form of SARFAESI is asked in virtually every exam paper — 1 mark, no room for error. Three pillars: Securitization + Asset Reconstruction + Enforcement. The third pillar (enforcement without court) is the most revolutionary and practically significant.

Key Definitions under SARFAESI
Non-Performing Asset (NPA)
A loan or advance where the borrower has stopped making interest or principal repayments. Under RBI prudential norms: a term loan becomes NPA if overdue for 90 days; an overdraft/cash credit if not cleared for 90 days; a bill if overdue for 90 days. SARFAESI applies only after NPA classification.
Secured Creditor — Section 2(zd)
Any bank, financial institution, or ARC in whose favour a security interest is created. SARFAESI rights are available ONLY to secured creditors. An unsecured lender (no collateral) cannot use Section 13 enforcement; must go to DRT/court.
Security Interest — Section 2(zf)
Right, title, or interest of any kind upon property created in favour of secured creditor. Includes: mortgage (immovable property), hypothecation (movable property — stock, machinery, vehicles), charge, assignment. Does NOT include pledge (where bank already has physical possession).
Financial Asset — Section 2(l)
A debt or receivable arising out of any loan or advance made by any bank or financial institution. Includes principal + interest due, NPAs, and securities (shares, bonds) held as collateral.
Asset Reconstruction Company (ARC)
A company registered with RBI under Section 3 of SARFAESI exclusively for asset reconstruction or securitization. Purchases NPAs from banks and attempts recovery. Must maintain minimum Net Owned Fund of ₹100 crore.
Security Receipt (SR)
An instrument issued by ARC/Securitization Company (SC) to Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs), backed by the pool of financial assets (loans) acquired from banks. SR holders receive cash flows as underlying loans are recovered.
Qualified Institutional Buyer (QIB)
A financial institution eligible to purchase Security Receipts: public financial institutions, insurance companies, banks, state financial corporations, mutual funds registered with SEBI, FIIs registered with SEBI, NBFCs with specified net worth.

Transactions/Assets Exempted from SARFAESI
  • Agricultural land (most critical exception): Banks CANNOT take possession of agricultural land under SARFAESI, regardless of loan amount. This is a major policy protection for farmers. Enforced through constitutional provisions and judicial interpretation.
  • Loans below ₹1 lakh: De minimis threshold — small loans must be recovered through civil courts/DRT

Securitization — Process, SPVs, Types & Funding

Securitization converts illiquid financial assets (pools of loans) into liquid tradable securities. This enables banks to: raise fresh capital, transfer credit risk, remove NPAs from balance sheets, and comply with capital adequacy requirements.

Step-by-Step Securitization Process
StepActionParty
1Bank identifies a pool of similar loans (e.g., 1000 home loans of ₹25–50 lakh each) for securitizationOriginator (Bank)
2Bank incorporates a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) — typically a trust — legally separate from the bankBank / SC/ARC
3Bank assigns/sells the loan pool to SPV at a negotiated (usually discounted) price; legal title to loans transfers to SPVOriginator → SPV
4SPV issues Security Receipts (SRs) or Pass-Through Certificates (PTCs) backed by the loan poolSPV
5SPV sells SRs/PTCs to Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) — mutual funds, insurance companies, banks (other than originator)SPV → QIBs
6Proceeds of SR sale go to originating bank — bank has effectively "sold" the loans and received cash upfrontQIBs → SPV → Bank
7As borrowers repay original loans, cash flows pass through SPV to SR/PTC holders proportionallyBorrowers → SPV → QIBs
Incorporation & Registration of SPVs
  • SPVs are incorporated as trusts or private limited companies specifically for one securitization transaction
  • Must be registered with RBI if conducting asset reconstruction business
  • SPV has bankruptcy remoteness — legally isolated from originating bank's financial distress
  • Each SPV maintains separate accounts and records for each securitization transaction
  • SC/ARC managing the SPV must submit periodic returns and reports to RBI
Types of Securitization Instruments
  • Pass-Through Certificates (PTCs): Most common in India. SR holders receive proportional cash flows as underlying borrowers repay. Principal + interest "passed through" to investors in proportion to their holding.
  • Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS): PTCs backed specifically by home loans; promoted by NHB (National Housing Bank) in India
    • Asset-Backed Securities (ABS): PTCs backed by auto loans, personal loans, microfinance loans, equipment finance loans
  • Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDOs): Complex structured products with tranches (senior/mezzanine/junior) with different risk-return profiles; less common in India
Funding of Securitization

SPVs fund the purchase of loan pools through:

  • Security Receipts (SRs) sold to QIBs: Primary funding mechanism; SRs are redeemed as underlying loans are recovered
  • Minimum 15% upfront cash payment: RBI's 2014 guidelines require ARCs to pay at least 15% of the NPA's agreed price in cash upfront; balance in SRs. (This was raised from an earlier lower threshold to ensure ARCs have "skin in the game")
  • Bank retains some SRs: The originating bank often retains a portion of SRs (typically junior tranches) to signal confidence in the asset quality and meet regulatory requirements
📌 Exam TipSecuritization process: Originator (Bank) → SPV → Security Receipts → QIBs. PTCs = pass-through certificates = most common in India. SPV has "bankruptcy remoteness" from bank. ARC minimum 15% upfront cash payment (2014 RBI guideline). MBS = home loans; ABS = other retail loans.

Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) & CERSAI
Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs)

ARCs are RBI-regulated specialized entities that purchase NPAs from banks at discounted prices and then deploy specialized skills to maximize recovery.

Registration & Capital Requirements
  • Must be incorporated as a company under Companies Act 2013
  • Must be registered with RBI under Section 3 of SARFAESI
  • Minimum Net Owned Fund (NOF): ₹100 crore (as per current RBI guidelines)
  • Cannot engage in any business other than asset reconstruction and securitization
  • Must maintain minimum Capital Adequacy and other RBI-prescribed financial parameters

  • JM Financial ARC: One of the largest by AUM
  • Edelweiss ARC
  • Phoenix ARC
  • Prudent ARC
  • Currently about 28 ARCs registered with RBI in India
Central Registry — CERSAI

CERSAI = Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest of India

  • Established under Section 20 of SARFAESI Act
  • Type: Government Company incorporated under Companies Act; RBI, NHB, and Central Government are promoters
  • Problem solved: Before CERSAI, fraudulent borrowers could mortgage the same property to multiple banks simultaneously. Banks had no way to check existing charges/mortgages before lending.
  • How it works: Whenever a bank creates a security interest (mortgage, hypothecation), it must register it on CERSAI within 30 days of creation
  • Any bank/institution can search CERSAI before granting a secured loan to check if the property/asset is already mortgaged or hypothecated
  • CERSAI now also covers: equitable mortgages (deposit of title deeds), security interests on movable property, assignments of receivables
  • Failure to register on CERSAI within 30 days does NOT invalidate the security interest as between parties, but can affect priority against third parties

Enforcement of Security Interest — Section 13 (Core Mechanism)

Section 13 is SARFAESI's most revolutionary and practically significant provision. It allows secured creditors to take action against defaulting borrowers WITHOUT approaching any court or tribunal — just a notice and waiting period.

Pre-conditions for Invoking Section 13
  • Loan must be classified as NPA per RBI prudential norms (90 days overdue)
  • Outstanding amount must be more than ₹1 lakh
  • Security must be a notified security interest — mortgage or hypothecation (NOT pledge)
  • Security is NOT agricultural land
  • The case is NOT otherwise exempted under Section 31

Four Methods of Enforcement — Section 13(4)
  • Method 1 — Take possession: Physical possession of secured assets (properties, machinery, vehicles, stock pledged as collateral)
  • Method 2 — Take over management: Take over the management of the borrower's secured business assets (appoint bank's nominee as manager)
  • Method 3 — Appoint Manager: Appoint any person to manage the secured assets on behalf of the bank — manages the business to preserve asset value pending sale
  • Method 4 — Require payment from third-party: Require any person who has acquired the secured asset from the borrower (after the charge was created) to pay the outstanding dues to the bank
Mardia Chemicals Ltd. vs. Union of India (2004) — Supreme Court

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of SARFAESI Act in this landmark case, but made the following important modifications:

  • Section 13(3A) was added mandatorily — bank MUST respond to borrower's objections with reasons within 15 days; it cannot simply ignore the representation
  • The original requirement of depositing 75% of dues before filing DRT appeal was struck down as unreasonable and disproportionate; current law requires 50% deposit (reducible to 25% by DRT)
  • The SC held that SARFAESI does not violate Articles 14, 19, or 300A of the Constitution (right to equality, freedom, and right to property)

Boiler Plate Provisions in SARFAESI Agreements

Every SARFAESI-related legal document (securitization agreement, ARC agreement, SR Trust Deed, assignment agreement) contains standard "boiler plate" clauses:

  • Governing Law clause: Specifies which country's / state's law governs interpretation and enforcement of the agreement
  • Jurisdiction clause: Specifies courts/tribunals (typically DRT or NCLT) that have exclusive or non-exclusive jurisdiction for disputes
  • Severability clause: If any provision is held invalid by a court, the rest of the agreement remains valid and enforceable
  • Force Majeure clause: Parties excused from obligations due to events beyond their control — pandemic, war, natural disaster, government action
  • Entire Agreement clause: The written document constitutes the complete understanding; prior oral or written representations not binding
  • Assignment clause: Specifies conditions under which rights and obligations can be transferred — critical for ARCs acquiring NPA portfolios
  • Indemnity clause: One party indemnifies the other against specified losses — bank indemnifies ARC for pre-existing third-party claims on assets sold
📌 Exam TipSection 36 = falsification of accounts = up to 5 years imprisonment (most severe SARFAESI penalty). Section 34 = wrongful possession / obstruction = up to 1 year. Boiler plate provisions are standard contract clauses — severability, force majeure, jurisdiction, governing law.

DRT — Debt Recovery Tribunal System
  • Established under Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act (RDDBFI Act), 1993
  • Jurisdiction: Bank/FI claims of ₹20 lakh and above
  • Bank files an Original Application (OA) before DRT for recovery of dues
  • DRT issues a Recovery Certificate (RC) in favour of the bank
  • RC is enforced by the Recovery Officer of DRT (who can attach and auction properties)
  • Appeal from DRT: to DRAT (Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal) within 30 days + 50% deposit
  • 33 DRTs and 5 DRATs across India
Legal Remedies for Banks & FIs
  • SARFAESI Section 13: Take possession of secured assets without court — fastest remedy for secured NPAs above ₹1 lakh
  • DRT (Section 19 RDDBFI Act): File OA for recovery of dues including unsecured portions; ≥₹20 lakh cases
  • IBC 2016 (NCLT): Corporate defaulters with ≥₹1 crore default; banks as Financial Creditors trigger CIRP; CoC-controlled resolution; max 270 days
  • Civil Suit: District Court for claims below ₹20 lakh (below DRT jurisdiction)

Quick Revision Summary

SARFAESI Act 2002 (recommended by Narasimham Committee II and Andhyarujina Committee) transformed India's NPA recovery landscape through three pillars: securitization (converting loan pools into tradable Security Receipts via SPVs for QIBs), asset reconstruction (ARCs purchasing NPAs at discounts — minimum NOF ₹100 crore; ARCIL = India's first ARC 2002), and enforcement of security interest without court orders (Section 13). Enforcement process: NPA classified → 60-day Demand Notice (Section 13(2)) to borrower + guarantors → borrower may object within 60 days; bank must respond within 15 days (Section 13(3A) — Mardia Chemicals 2004) → if unpaid, bank takes possession/management using 4 methods (Section 13(4)) → borrower appeals to DRT within 45 days with 50% deposit → DRT → DRAT within 30 days → High Court. CERSAI (Central Registry, Section 20) prevents fraudulent multiple mortgages by requiring 30-day registration of all security interests. Key exclusions: agricultural land (most important), loans below ₹1 lakh, pledge of movables. Most severe penalty: 5 years imprisonment for falsification of accounts (Section 36). SARFAESI works alongside DRT (≥₹20 lakh, RDDBFI 1993) and IBC 2016 (≥₹1 crore corporate, NCLT-based CIRP max 270 days, only system with automatic moratorium) as India's comprehensive three-pronged NPA resolution framework.
